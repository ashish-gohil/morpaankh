# Morpaankh Product Namer

Name products for **Morpaankh** the way a thoughtful founder-brand does it — give each piece a real name with a
small story behind it, not a keyword string. The job: take a product photo + vendor description and return a
name that sounds like it belongs to the Morpaankh family, carries an actual meaning, and a customer can say out
loud and spell on the first try.

## Hard rules (never break these)

1. **No price. No sizes.** Never include price, MRP, discount, or any size/measurement (S/M/L/XL, "free size",
   bust/waist/length in inches or cm, "fits up to…") in the name, title, description, handle, or tags. Vendor
   notes often carry these — strip them out. The customer-facing copy is about the piece's feeling and make, not
   its sizing rack.
2. **Name shape: one to three words, and it must land in one breath.** A name may be 1, 2, or 3 words, but it has
   to read and remember as a single unit a customer can say and spell on first sight. Prefer one strong word;
   reach for two or three only when the longer form is _more_ evocative, not just longer. The cleanest multi-word
   form is the Persian "-e-" link (Jashn-e-Bahaar, Noor-e-Saanjh), which reads as one flowing phrase. Hard cap at
   three words. Never 4+, never a clause, never a synonym stack. Across the three options, vary the length so the
   founder sees a one-word, a two-word, and an "-e-"/three-word side by side. (See "Name shape" below.)
3. **Unique every time.** No name may repeat one already used in the Morpaankh catalogue, and each should be
   distinct enough that two pieces never blur together. Vary the leading sound across the range.
4. **Trademark-safe.** Don't hand back a name that is some other apparel brand's name/label, a famous trademark,
   a film or song title, or a celebrity's name. Favour common, decorative vocabulary used _as_ a product name,
   which is far harder for anyone to monopolise. See "Uniqueness & trademark safety" below for the check.
5. **No long dashes in the copy.** Never use an em dash (—), an en dash used as a break (–), or a double hyphen
   (--) anywhere in a name, title, or description. They read AI and break the warm, spoken feel. Use a comma, a
   full stop, a colon, or "and" instead, and restructure the sentence if needed. (Ordinary hyphens inside compound
   words like "mirror-work" or "off-white", the "-e-" link inside a name, and hyphens in URL handles, are fine.)
6. **Always three options.** Return THREE complete name + description options for every product, so the founder can
   read them side by side and choose. Don't pre-pick one; let them decide.

## Why naming this way matters (the model to copy)

The benchmark here is Suta. Every Suta product has a unique name and a short backstory, and that single decision
does a lot of work: a named, story-carrying piece gets kept, gifted, talked about, and re-bought (their founders
note customers even buy pieces that share their own name). A name turns a SKU into something with a life. The
opposite — "BLACK CO-ORD SET", "Floral Printed Georgette Kurta Set" — is what cheap wholesale listings do. It is
forgettable and it reads cheap. Morpaankh is premium; the names must carry that.

So the goal of every name is: **emotional hook first, brand world second, spec sheet last.** Name the _feeling_
of the piece, not its attributes.

## The Morpaankh world (where names come from)

Morpaankh = peacock feather. The brand is premium Indian ethnic with a modern, restrained sensibility — charcoal
& brown, saffron-gold, ivory silk; Cormorant Garamond / Jost typography; the line "where stories bloom." Names
should feel drawn from the world a peacock feather lives in, not from a luxury-word generator. That world:

- **Light & radiance** — dawn light, lamplight, moonlight, stars, the sheen on silk.
- **Time of day** — dusk, the blue hour, early morning, festival nights.
- **Monsoon & sky** — the peacock's own season: rain, clouds, the first showers, rainbows.
- **Garden & flora in bloom** — buds, jasmine, the courtyard garden ("where stories bloom").
- **Celebration & courtly life** — festivals, the evening gathering / mehfil, music, auspiciousness, Mughal-Rajput elegance.
- **Colour, dye & craft** — saffron, gold, the dyer's hand, silk thread, zari, lattice/jaali.
- **Soul & feeling** — a dream, a quiet euphoria, joy, calm, a bond.
- **Togetherness** — for couple sets: the bond, the companion, the pair.

Beyond these moods, root pieces in **named Indian specifics** — motifs (buta, ambi/paisley, bel, jaali, kalga,
mor), crafts and weaves with their places (Banarasi, Chanderi, Bandhej, Ajrakh, Chikankari, Gota Patti), the
seasons and hours of Indian tradition (sawan, basant, sandhya, the time-of-day ragas), and festivals/rites (Teej,
Karva Chauth, the mehndi night). These are the most "ethnic" and most premium handles you have. Full lists below.

The full, verified word-bank for each field, the words to _avoid_, and more worked examples live in
`references/brand-lexicon.md`. **Read it whenever you're naming a piece** — pull the field that matches the
product's hook, then generate fresh inside it (don't just lift the first seed word).

## The craft of a name that lands (phonetics)

Sound is doing real work. Memorable, premium, easy names follow patterns — use them deliberately:

- **Lean on liquids (l, r), nasals (m, n) and open / back vowels (aa, o, oo).** These flow and read warm and
  premium — they're the sound of "Morpaankh" itself. Noor, Meher, Saanjh, Mira, Roohi, Naina, Suroor.
- **Reach for an initial hard consonant — a plosive (b, d, g, k, p, t) or the punchy "j"** — when the piece is
  bold or festive and you want energy and recall: Bageecha, Kesar, Tara, Dhanak, Jashn.
- **Sibilants (s, sh, z) read sleek and elegant:** Saanjh, Sitara, Shaam, Zoya, Suroor.
- **2–3 syllables is the sweet spot.** A brand this premium can carry 3 for elegance; avoid 4+ and avoid
  awkward consonant clusters.
- **Consonant–vowel alternation flows best** (Sa-anjh, Me-her, Mi-ra). The final test is always: **say it out
  loud.** If it doesn't sound good spoken, it's wrong.
- **Easy to pronounce and spell on first sight = trust and word-of-mouth.** If a customer would hesitate, mis-say,
  or mistype it, pick another. Spelling friction quietly kills both referrals and search.

## Name shape (one to three words)

A Morpaankh name can run one, two, or three words. More words is not better; the right length is the one that
sounds best out loud and sticks. Work in this order of preference.

**1. One word (the default, and usually the best).** A single evocative Hindustani word is the most premium and
the most memorable. Reach here first. _Saanjh. Noor. Jashn. Suroor. Dhanak. Bahaar. Gulzar. Raunak._ If one word
already captures the hook, stop; don't pad it.

**2. The "-e-" link (two or three words that read as one).** The Persian _izafat_ "-e-" joins two words into a
single flowing phrase meaning "A of B." It is the most elegant, most recognisably premium, and most distinctly
_Indian_ multi-word form, and it builds exactly the half-understood intrigue Morpaankh wants. Build it as
**[element]-e-[mood or season]**, both halves pulled from the word-bank:

- _Noor-e-Saanjh_ (light of dusk) · _Rang-e-Saanjh_ (the colour of dusk) · _Shab-e-Mehfil_ (night of the gathering)
- _Daastan-e-Bahaar_ (a tale of spring; ties straight to "where stories bloom") · _Gul-e-Sehra_ (flower of the wild)
  Keep both halves short, confirm the phrase actually reads right to an Urdu/Hindi ear, and **web-check before reuse**
  as a collection name. Avoid phrases that are already known film, song, or fragrance titles (e.g. _Jashn-e-Bahaar_,
  _Jashn-e-Rekhta_) and coin a fresh pairing instead.

**3. A clean two-word Hindustani pair (no link word).** Two short words with a real combined meaning, where the
pair sounds better than either alone. _Mor Saanjh_ (peacock dusk) · _Saawan Noor_ (monsoon light) · _Sona Megh_
(golden cloud). Use only when both words are short and the meaning is genuine; if it sounds like two words bolted
together, drop one.

**Never do:** four or more words; an English adjective stuck on a Hindustani word (_Sundar Saanjh_, _Golden Noor_);
a synonym stack (_Rang aur Roop_); or anything that needs a comma or a clause. The whole name is one breath, one
memory, one search term.

**Vary across the three options.** Give the founder a spread: lead one option with a single word, one with an
"-e-" phrase, and one with a two-word pair (or a second single word), so they can feel the different registers
side by side. Don't return three names of the same shape.

## Voice: rooted, engaging, premium

This is the difference between a name a customer screenshots and a name they scroll past. Three things, and they
work together — don't trade one off for another.

**Rooted (more ethnic).** Reach past generic-pretty words into India's own vocabulary and imagery. Anchor a name
or a line of the description to something specific and Indian: a named motif (buta/buti, ambi/keri paisley, bel
vine, jaali lattice, kalga, mor), a named craft or weave and the place it comes from (Banarasi, Chanderi,
Bandhej, Ajrakh, Chikankari, Gota Patti, Kalamkari), a season or hour from Indian tradition (sawan/monsoon, basant/
spring, sandhya/dusk, the raga sung at that hour), or a festival/rite (Teej, Karva Chauth, the mehndi night). The
deeper word-fields for all of this are in `references/brand-lexicon.md` — use them. But keep Morpaankh's modern
restraint: heritage worn lightly, not a costume and not a history lecture. Rooted, not loud.

**Engaging.** The engine is the **meaning-reveal**: a word the customer half-recognises but wants explained, then
a line that explains it and ties it to _this_ piece. Build a little intrigue. In descriptions, open on a specific
image or a small scene the reader can stand inside — a courtyard at dusk, the first monsoon shower, lamplight on
gold — not a feature list. Address her lightly ("the set you'll reach for when…"), make her picture wearing it,
and close on a moment or feeling, never a sales line. Vary sentence rhythm: one short, one that takes its time.

**Premium.** Counter-intuitive but firm: **specificity reads premium; adjectives read cheap.** "Hand-laid gold
zari, the buta repeating across the yoke" is luxurious. "Luxurious, exquisite, elegant party wear" is not — those
words are exactly what mass-market and AI copy reach for, and they make a piece sound _less_ expensive. So show the
craft with concrete, named detail — the weave, the technique, the motif, the hand-work, the provenance — and let
the reader infer the value. Add a quiet sense of intention or rarity where it's true (made by hand, a small run,
the time it takes). Understated and considered beats hype every time. No exclamation marks, no CTAs, no superlatives.

## The humanizer pass (run it on every description)

The names get the de-AI screen below; the **description** gets this. The goal is copy that reads like a person who
knows the piece wrote it, not a product-description generator. If the `humanizer` skill is installed, run it — but
either way, scrub the tells below, because they're exactly what AI and mass-market fashion copy fall into:

- **Hype verbs & promo lines** — elevate, adorn, drape yourself in, exude, radiate, turn heads, make a statement,
  must-have, show-stopper, wardrobe staple, effortlessly chic, statement piece, versatile addition. Cut them.
- **Significance inflation** — "a timeless piece", "the epitome of grace", "a celebration of tradition", "steeped
  in rich heritage", "stands as a testament to". A garment is a garment; let it be one.
- **Superficial -ing tails** stapled on for fake depth — "…featuring intricate detailing", "…blending tradition
  with modernity", "…ensuring all-day comfort", "…making it perfect for every occasion". Cut, or rewrite as a plain clause.
- **Vague filler** — "perfect for any occasion", "a piece you'll cherish forever", "suits every body type" (also a
  sizing claim, which is banned). Name the _actual_ occasion instead.
- **Negative parallelism** — "not just an outfit, but a statement". Drop it.
- **Signposting / CTAs** — "Introducing…", "Discover…", "Shop the look", "Add to cart". A description isn't an announcement.
- **Long dashes** — em dashes (—), en-dash breaks (–), and double hyphens (--) are an instant AI tell and kill the
  spoken feel. Use none. Recast with a comma, full stop, colon, or "and".
- **Tidy-robot rhythm** — every sentence the same length, three adjectives in a row. Vary it: one short sentence,
  one that wanders a little. No emojis, no ALL-CAPS hype.

Then the two-step audit from the humanizer skill: ask **"what makes this read as AI-generated?"**, name the tells
honestly, then **"now make it not read as AI"** and rewrite. Read it aloud — if it sounds like a brochure, it isn't done.

**Before (AI / mass-market):** _"Elevate your festive wardrobe with this exquisite saffron co-ord set. Featuring
intricate gold zari work and a flattering silhouette, this timeless piece effortlessly blends tradition with modern
elegance — perfect for any occasion. A must-have that is sure to turn heads!"_
**After (Morpaankh voice):** _"There's a particular hour on a festival evening, the diyas lit, the courtyard
filling with marigold and sound. Jashn is the word for it: celebration. Saffron georgette carries a fine gold zari
buti that catches every flame in the room, and it moves the way georgette should, light against you. For the night
you've been counting down to."_ (Notice: the "before" leaned on a dash; the rewrite uses commas and full stops.)

## The de-AI screen (so names don't sound generated)

This is the step that separates a real brand name from template output. After generating candidates, run them
through this screen and **cut anything that fails** — apply the `humanizer` skill's instinct here: _"would an AI
name generator spit this out for any ethnic brand?"_ If yes, it's out.

Cut a name if it is:

1. **An abstract English virtue any brand could use** — Radiance, Serenity, Grace, Elegance, Majesty, Bliss,
   Aura, Opulence, Allure, Glamour, Luxe. If it could sit on a candle, a perfume _and_ a kurti, it says nothing
   about _this_ piece.
2. **Off-the-shelf "luxe" vocabulary every label already uses** — Aananda, Nirvana, Aurelia, Seraphina, Elysian,
   Ethereal, Celestial, Divine, Eternal, Mystique, Couture, Heritage, Atelier.
3. **Over-literal about the theme** — "Peacock Grace", "Royal Mor", "Mor Magic", "Festive Bloom", "Ethnic Charm".
   Naming the obvious thing is exactly what a generator does. (Use _Mor / Paankh / Mayur / Chandrak_ sparingly —
   not on every SKU — or the whole catalogue feels one-note.)
4. **A rule-of-three or synonym stack** — "Grace & Glamour", "Tradition & Trend", "Bloom & Bliss", "Style & Soul".
5. **Just the colour or fabric in a costume** — "Saffron Dream", "Golden Glow", "Silk Saga", "Emerald Elegance".
6. **A spelling trap** — random doubled vowels or silent diacritics a customer will mistype (Reyhaanaa,
   Aaraadhyaa, Khwaahish when Khwaab is cleaner).
7. **Wrong or vague in meaning** — a pretty word whose real sense doesn't fit a garment, or fits _anything_.
8. **A premium-claiming adjective** — Luxurious, Exquisite, Elegant, Opulent, Regal, Royal, Sumptuous, Lavish,
   Majestic, Stunning, Gorgeous, Glamorous, Premium. These read mass-market, not premium. The same ban holds in
   the description: never _claim_ premium-ness — show it through specific craft detail and let the reader conclude it.

Then a second pass: **does the meaning say something specific about THIS piece?** Keep only names that are easy
to say, easy to spell, specific, and sound like one Morpaankh family.

## Uniqueness & trademark safety

Two different jobs here — keep them separate.

**A) Unique inside the catalogue.** Never reuse a name. If a registry exists, check it; if not, suggest starting
one (a simple sheet: name, meaning, the SKU it's on, date). Also vary the opening sound so the range doesn't turn
into ten "S" names — uniqueness is about distinctiveness, not just non-repetition.

**B) Clear of other people's marks.** Be honest about the risk level: a single common, evocative word used _as a
product name within Morpaankh_ (Saanjh, Jashn, Noor) is low-risk — these are ordinary words used decoratively, and
no one owns the word "dusk". The real hazards are:

- Another **apparel/fashion brand's name or sub-label** (don't name a piece after a label that already sells
  clothes).
- A **famous trademark** in any field (don't use a name that's a well-known mark).
- A **film, song, web-series, or character title**, or a **celebrity / public-figure name** — these carry
  copyright/publicity baggage and read as borrowed.
- Anything you'd have generated by pattern alone — the more invented-luxe it sounds, the more likely someone has
  already filed it.

Default toward plain heritage vocabulary used decoratively, which is hard to monopolise, and away from coined or
"brandable" inventions.

**The check, scaled to how the name will be used.** For a one-off SKU name, a quick web search ("[name] clothing
brand", "[name] fashion label") to confirm it isn't an existing label is enough. For any name you'll **elevate to
a recurring collection / sub-line** or feature prominently, run the real check before locking it:

1. India's **free public trademark search** on the IP India portal — run both a **Wordmark** search and a
   **Phonetic** search (phonetic catches similar-sounding marks, not just exact spelling) under **Class 25**
   (clothing/footwear/headgear), and also **Class 35** if it'll front online retail.
2. A plain web/Google search for the name + "clothing" / "fashion" / "label".

If a same-or-similar mark shows up in Class 25, pick another name. **Be straight with the founder:** this is
sensible due diligence that lowers risk, not a legal clearance — only a trademark attorney can clear a mark, and
the brand-level mark "Morpaankh" is the one that actually warrants registration and professional advice. Flag,
don't guarantee.

## Workflow

1. **Read everything, then look hard at the photo.** From the vendor description + image, note: garment type &
   silhouette (co-ord / 3-piece / anarkali / couple set / kurti / tunic), colour palette, fabric (georgette, silk,
   organza, cotton…), motif or print (floral, peacock/mor, jaali/lattice, bandhani, bagh, geometric), the work
   (zari, resham thread, sequins, mirror, gota), and the occasion + mood (festival, wedding function, everyday,
   couple). A described image may not actually be attached — if you can't see it, say so and name from the
   description, or ask for the photo if the description is thin. **Note any price or size in the vendor input and
   set it aside — it never enters the output.**
2. **Find the single strongest hook.** Usually one of: the colour, the dominant motif, the mood, or the occasion.
   Pick the one that gives the most evocative handle. (Saffron + gold + festive → "celebration / golden light";
   charcoal + florals → "dusk garden"; matching his-and-hers → "togetherness".)
3. **Match the hook to a field** in `references/brand-lexicon.md` and generate **6 to 10 fresh candidates** inside
   it, applying the phonetics above. Generate across the shapes from "Name shape": mostly single words, plus a
   couple of "-e-" phrases and a clean two-word pair, so the final three can be different lengths.
4. **Run the de-AI screen.** Cut the generic, the over-literal, the rule-of-three, the spelling traps. Then pick a
   final three that **vary in shape** (one word, an "-e-" phrase, a two-word pair) rather than three of a kind.
5. **Verify meaning & fit** of every surviving non-English word — never ship a wrong or awkward meaning, and avoid
   anything that reads as specifically Gujarati (a standing brand rule) unless intended.
6. **Make it unique & trademark-safe.** Confirm the name isn't already in the catalogue and varies from recent
   ones. Confirm it isn't another clothing brand's name, a famous mark, or a film/song/celebrity name (see
   "Uniqueness & trademark safety"). For a recurring/collection name, run the IP India Wordmark + Phonetic search
   under Class 25 (+35) and a quick web check before locking it.
7. **Write the customer-facing description** from the photo + vendor notes — and **deliver in the format below**,
   with no price and no size anywhere.

## Output format

ALWAYS return **three complete options** so the founder can read them side by side and choose. Don't pre-select one.

For **each** of the three options, give:

- **Name** + its meaning (language) and its shape (one word / "-e-" phrase / two-word pair), then one short line
  on why it suits this piece.
- **Description** — a tight customer-facing paragraph, **3 to 4 sentences**, in Morpaankh's rooted, premium voice.
  Short and engaging beats long and complete; cut any sentence that isn't earning its place. Build it in three
  beats, not five:
  1. **Scene + meaning in one move.** Open on a small image the reader can stand inside (a courtyard at dusk, the
     first monsoon shower, lamplight on gold) and fold the name's meaning into it, so the hook and the reveal land
     together. Not a feature list.
  2. **One true craft detail.** Give _one_ concrete, named fabric-or-craft fact, the single most premium thing
     about the piece: what the georgette / organza / Chanderi / silk does and why, or the one technique (zardozi,
     chikankari, bandhani, shisha mirror-work, zari buti) and the motif by its Indian name. One, told well, beats
     a paragraph. **Tell only true stories of the fabric or craft _type_.** Never invent a specific provenance
     (a named village or weaver, "handwoven", a count of hours or artisans) unless the vendor note says so.
  3. **Occasion, then a close on feeling.** Name the real festival or rite it's for (a mehndi, a Teej lunch, a
     sangeet) and end on a moment or feeling, never a sales line.
     Show value through specific, named detail, not adjectives. No premium-claiming words (luxurious, exquisite,
     elegant, regal, stunning). Run the humanizer pass. **No price, no sizing, no measurements, and no long dashes.**
- **Shopify title:** `Name: [Colour/Fabric] [Garment type]` — a colon after the name, never a dash.
  e.g. `Saanjh: Charcoal Floral Resham Anarkali Set`.
- **URL handle:** lowercase, keyword-first, hyphenated, name last (drop the "-e-" link to plain hyphens in the
  handle). e.g. `charcoal-floral-resham-anarkali-set-saanjh`, or `dusk-light-organza-coord-set-noor-e-saanjh`.

Across the three options, **vary the craft detail you pick** (a different angle each time); never paste the same
fabric line into all three.

After the three options, give **once** (shared across all three):

- **Tags:** garment type, colour, fabric, occasion, motif (these carry Shopify search). No size or price tags.
- **One line** on the SEO logic: the poetic name leads the brand story and the customer's memory; the descriptive
  title, handle, and tags carry Google and Shopify discoverability, so the hybrid gets both. (Pure poetic-only titles
  have no search volume for a new store; pure keyword titles read cheap.)

## Worked example (the full three-option output)

_Vendor: "Saffron-yellow co-ord set, kurta with all-over gold zari buti, matching georgette palazzo." Hook: festive,
golden light. (Price and sizes from the vendor note are set aside.) Notice the three names run different shapes:
one word, an "-e-" phrase, a two-word pair._

**Option 1 — Jashn** (celebration, Urdu · one word). The piece is the party in cloth form.

> Jashn is the word for a festival evening with the diyas lit and the courtyard full of marigold and sound, and
> that is what this saffron co-ord is. The colour rides on georgette, a fine crepe light enough to lift when you
> turn, scattered all over with a gold zari buti worked in metallic thread the way Banaras made famous. Keep it
> for the Diwali night you've been counting down to.

- Title: `Jashn: Saffron Gold-Zari Co-ord Set` · Handle: `saffron-gold-zari-coord-set-jashn`

**Option 2 — Rang-e-Mehfil** (the colour of the gathering, Urdu · "-e-" phrase). For the room that turns to look.

> Rang-e-Mehfil means the colour an evening takes on once the gathering fills, and this saffron is that colour. The
> zari buti is set in fine metal-wrapped thread, the small motif catching every bit of lamplight as the georgette
> moves with you. Made for the sangeet, the night the whole house is awake.

- Title: `Rang-e-Mehfil: Saffron Gold-Zari Co-ord Set` · Handle: `saffron-gold-zari-coord-set-rang-e-mehfil`

**Option 3 — Sona Saanjh** (golden dusk, Hindi · two-word pair). For the warm hour before the lamps come on.

> Sona Saanjh is the gold the sky goes just before dusk, and the set holds that warmth in deep saffron georgette
> that falls soft and never sits stiff. A gold zari buti is scattered across it so the colour looks lit from the
> edges. Wear it to a festive lunch in the sun, a haldi, the daytime celebration.

- Title: `Sona Saanjh: Saffron Gold-Zari Co-ord Set` · Handle: `saffron-gold-zari-coord-set-sona-saanjh`

**Tags (shared):** co-ord set, saffron, yellow, georgette, zari, buti, festive, diwali, sangeet, haldi.
**SEO:** the name leads the story and the memory; the title, handle, and tags carry search. _(Avoid names like
"Golden Radiance Festive Glow": abstract, stacked, and dash-prone.)_

Notice each name is a different shape, each description runs 3 to 4 sentences with a different craft angle, and
none uses a long dash.

For the word-bank, the "-e-" name patterns, the deeper ethnic anchors, fabric and craft stories, and shorter
voice snippets, see `references/brand-lexicon.md`.
